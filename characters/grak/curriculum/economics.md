# Economics

> Grak wider canon seed 9 of 9. The clan half of the coin-logic side: incentives, aggregates, and why the obvious policy produces the opposite result.

**Why the house keeps it.** Every system a builder ships is an incentive machine: pricing pages, rate limits, retry budgets, on-call rotations. Economics is the study of what people and aggregates do after the incentive changes, which is never what the diagram promised.

## Canon

- **Adam Smith**, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): division of labor, markets as coordination, the invisible hand as a mechanism (not a morality).
- **David Ricardo** (1817): comparative advantage; trade gains from relative, not absolute, cost.
- **Thomas Malthus**: population and diminishing returns; the first growth pessimist.
- **John Stuart Mill**: synthesis; the limits of the wage fund; liberty as policy.
- **Karl Marx**, *Das Kapital* (1867): capital accumulation and crisis; read as analysis, not prophecy.
- **Alfred Marshall**, *Principles of Economics* (1890): supply and demand curves; marginal analysis; elasticity.
- **John Maynard Keynes**, *The General Theory* (1936): aggregate demand; sticky wages; the state as demand of last resort.
- **Friedrich Hayek**: prices as dispersed information; the knowledge problem; spontaneous order.
- **Milton Friedman**: monetarism; "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon"; the natural rate.
- **Paul Samuelson**: the mathematical synthesis; comparative statics as the workhorse.
- **Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu**: general equilibrium; the existence theorem and its fine print.
- **George Akerlof**: information asymmetry; the market for lemons.
- **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky**: prospect theory; loss aversion; the descriptive turn.
- **Elinor Ostrom**: commons governance; the tragedy is not inevitable, institutions are the variable.

## Working toolkit

- **Micro.** Scarcity and opportunity cost; marginal thinking (decide at the margin, not the average); supply/demand equilibrium; shifts vs movements along a curve.
- **Elasticity.** Price elasticity = %ΔQ / %ΔP; income and cross elasticities; when |E| > 1 a price cut raises revenue; taxable incidence falls on the less elastic side.
- **Surplus and welfare.** Consumer and producer surplus; deadweight loss from taxes, price floors, and ceilings; externalities and public goods; Coase and transaction costs.
- **Market structure.** Perfect competition, monopoly (deadweight loss, price discrimination), oligopoly (Cournot, Bertrand), monopolistic competition; entry and exit as the long-run correction.
- **Game theory.** Payoff matrices; dominant strategies; Nash equilibrium; prisoner's dilemma; repeated games and tit-for-tat; credible threats and commitment.
- **Macro aggregates.** GDP (nominal vs real; deflator = nominal/real); GDP = C + I + G + (X − M); inflation (CPI vs PPI); real vs nominal rates (Fisher: r ≈ i − π); unemployment types (frictional, structural, cyclical); labor force participation.
- **Models.** AS/AD; the multiplier (1/(1 − MPC)); the Phillips curve and its instability (expectations move it); the quantity theory MV = PY; growth accounting (capital, labor, productivity).
- **Money and policy.** Central bank balance sheet; policy rate; open market operations; reserve requirements; QE; transmission lags; fiscal stance; crowding out; seigniorage; deflation debt spiral.
- **International.** Comparative advantage and opportunity-cost tables; tariffs and quotas as taxes with deadweight loss; terms of trade; exchange rates; PPP (long-run anchor); interest parity; balance of payments; current account as savings minus investment.
- **Schools.** Classical (flexible prices), Keynesian (demand), monetarist (money), new classical (rational expectations), Austrian (capital structure and malinvestment), behavioral (biases), institutional (rules and commons). Each is a model with a domain; none is the whole map.

## Method

1. Find the incentive before naming the intent. Who is paid, who pays, and what happens at the margin.
2. Draw supply and demand and label which curve shifts and why. "Price rose" is not an analysis.
3. Check incidence: the legal payer and the economic payer are usually different.
4. Run the counterfactual: compared to what? Policy without a baseline is a story.
5. Watch lags and expectations: the effect arrives late, and people anticipate.

## Failure modes

- Composition fallacy: what is true for one firm is not true for all firms (paradox of thrift).
- Broken window: counting the repair as a gain and ignoring the unseen alternative.
- Post hoc policy inference: the recession ended after the stimulus; also, it might have ended anyway. Friedman's thermostat: policy responds to the economy, so correlation runs both ways.
- Zero-sum trade thinking; comparative advantage is not a contest of absolute productivity.
- Ignoring opportunity cost or the unseen alternative (Bastiat's seen vs unseen).
- Confusing correlation with causation in aggregates; omitted variables and reverse causality everywhere.
- Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring.
- Treating CPI as a personal cost-of-living index; your basket is not the basket.
- Natural-rate denial in either direction: printing cannot lower unemployment forever, and economies can stay below potential for years.
